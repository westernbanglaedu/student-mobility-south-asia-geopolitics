Why India is Underutilized by Bangladeshi Students

Published: March 24, 2025 12 min read

India is the closest large higher education system to Bangladesh by geography, culture, and language. Yet the current evidence suggests it captures only a fraction of Bangladesh's outward student demand. Understanding why requires looking at both the opportunity and the barriers.

Against a total outbound figure of 52,799 students in 2023, the 2,606 AISHE enrollment represents approximately 4.9% of Bangladesh's outward mobility. This share seems low given that India is Bangladesh's largest neighbor, shares cultural and linguistic familiarity, and offers accessible medical education.

Measurement Note

The 2,606 figure comes from AISHE institutional enrollment data and reflects students on roll in Indian institutions — an active stock measure rather than annual outbound flow. The actual annual movement from Bangladesh to India may be higher, but the AISHE figure establishes a minimum visible presence that underperforms the corridor's theoretical potential.

Why India Should Be Higher

Several structural factors suggest India should capture more of Bangladesh's outbound demand:

Geographic Proximity

India shares a 4,096-kilometer border with Bangladesh — one of the longest international land borders in the world. Travel costs between major cities in both countries are substantially lower than costs to destinations in Europe, North America, or Oceania. For families monitoring expenses over a multi-year degree program, proximity translates directly into lower visit costs and easier return access.

Cultural and Linguistic Familiarity

Bangladeshi students encounter less cultural adjustment in India than in most other major study destinations. Shared historical context, Bengali language presence in West Bengal and parts of Northeast India, and familiarity with Indian media create a more navigable environment for first-time international students.

Medical Education Accessibility

India offers a well-established medical education system with English-medium instruction in many institutions. For Bangladeshi students seeking medical careers — a significant driver of outbound mobility — India's system provides a recognizable pathway with regionally appropriate clinical exposure.

Cost Structure

While not uniformly low-cost, India's private medical and professional education often carries lower total program costs than equivalent qualifications in Western countries. The cost-accessibility ratio makes India competitive for students whose primary goal is degree completion rather than elite institutional prestige.

Why the Gap Exists

Despite these advantages, several factors limit Bangladesh-to-India student mobility:

Recognition Concerns

Uncertainty about whether Indian degrees meet Bangladesh's professional licensing requirements creates hesitation. Students need assurance that their investment produces usable credentials.

Information Asymmetry

Bangladeshi students and families often lack clear, accessible information about Indian institution quality, program structures, and application processes compared to information available for Western destinations.

Perceived Political Risk

Despite shared border proximity, political tension between the two countries affects perceived safety. Students may worry about visa complications or sudden policy changes affecting their enrollment status.

Destination Diversification

Bangladesh's outbound market has expanded globally. Students increasingly view destinations beyond South Asia as offering better long-term returns through immigration pathways and global credential value.

What Would Change the Equation

The evidence suggests that India could capture a larger share of Bangladesh's outbound market if several conditions improved:

  1. Credential recognition clarity: Explicit mutual recognition agreements for medical and professional degrees would reduce the biggest source of hesitation for Bangladeshi students and families.
  2. Institutional visibility: Indian universities need stronger outreach in Bangladesh to compete with established Western recruitment networks that have been operating for decades.
  3. Scholarship pathways: Visible scholarship programs specifically targeting Bangladeshi students would lower cost barriers and signal institutional commitment to the market.
  4. Alumni networks: Visible success stories of Bangladeshi graduates from Indian institutions would provide social proof that currently exists only at smaller scale.
Key Insight

The Bangladesh-India corridor is not broken — it is underinvested. Students use India when pathways are clear and when institutional actors make the opportunity visible. The gap between potential and actual utilization represents a market opportunity, not a structural failure.

The Pattern Beyond Bangladesh

The underutilization question extends beyond Bangladesh. The broader white paper evidence shows that India as a destination does not dominate any single outbound market in South Asia consistently, despite its regional position. This suggests that India's soft power advantage in higher education — geography, culture, cost — is not automatically converting into student mobility.

The implication is that institutional action matters more than structural advantage. Countries and institutions that actively recruit, provide clear information, and build credential recognition create mobility channels. India's higher education system has the capacity, but the conversion from potential to actual enrollment requires deliberate market development that the current evidence suggests has not fully occurred.
